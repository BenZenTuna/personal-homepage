Kitchen wall

An old laptop screwed flat to the kitchen wall, lid open, one cable out of the corner, running all day. It turns the house lights on, works out what is for dinner, pulls ten years of photographs off the NAS in the cupboard, and watches the birds nesting on the balcony. This is that same interface with a made-up server behind it — every reading on the page is invented, and nothing here reaches a house.

  • The lights, from the wall. Every room the house has a bulb in, through Home Assistant and the Hue bridge — one tap a room, one tap the lot — so nobody has to find a phone and wait for an app while carrying a hot pan.
  • Dinner, worked out rather than looked up. Photographs go to an AI agent — the open fridge, the shelves, a page of a recipe book, the supermarket receipt — and what comes back is a week of dinners, one vegetarian and one not for every day, cooked from what is already in the cupboards. Today's two are on the cards; Week is the rest of the plan, and one tap puts everything it still needs on the shopping list. Choose a dinner and it lays the cooking out as steps, one at a time, big enough to read from across the kitchen with both hands full.
  • It sleeps when the kitchen is empty. The wall is a Home Assistant client like any other: a Hue motion sensor sees somebody walk in and the screen comes back, and when the room has been still a while it goes dark. A lit screen nobody is standing at is a lamp pointed at the ceiling.
  • Everything the house has ever photographed. The pictures and videos live on the NAS in the cupboard, and the wall reaches into them all day — a few hundred pulled across at a time and shown at random, so a decade of holidays and small children keeps surfacing between the forecast and the shopping list. The ♥ keeps one on the server, so it goes on playing with the NAS asleep; the 👎 takes one out of the show for good and writes its path down for deleting later.
  • The birds on the balcony. A camera inside the nest box out there, streamed to the panel. It watches for movement on its own chip and tells the wall: the block along the top bar is how many times something has come to the box today and the last three stills of it, and behind that is every one of them, steppable with ‹ ›. The wall can chime when it reports and flash the lights in whichever rooms are too far away to hear it. The clip looping in this demo is not that stream: it is AI-generated, a stand-in for what the box will show when the new nesting season comes round, and the stills in the log are frames cut out of it.

Looking around this copy

  • Six views along the top — Everything, Cooking, House, Photos, Birds, TV — each in the colour its own panels use, and the gear beside them rearranges the one you are on.
  • Everything is touch-first. Nothing is smaller than a fingertip and there are no hover states. Any panel takes the whole screen with ⤢, and carries the clock, the weather and the views up with it — the six buttons stay at the same place on the glass in every view.
  • Tap the temperature for the whole report: the next two days hour by hour beside the next ten days. The bird box block opens the birds, and one of its stills opens that still. The clock is timers and world clocks.
  • It answers. Lights toggle and dim, dinners can be chosen and rated, timers run, ingredients tick off onto a shopping list, the bird box will report if you ask it to (Test motion, behind the gear on Birds), and a kept photograph stays kept — all of it held in your browser and thrown away by Reset.

You are on a small screen, and this is drawn for a 1920×1080 wall. It still works — turn the phone sideways, or open it on a laptop to see it at the size it was designed for.

The photographs are the house's own; the bird box clip is AI-generated, and the stills in its log are frames cut out of that clip. Everything else — the weather, the rooms, the dinners, the week, the events, the channels, every visit to the box — is invented. No live data, no accounts, nothing leaves this page.
